# Chapter I: The Nature of Intelligence

![A barn owl perched on a weathered branch](images/barn-owl.jpg)

*The Owl: Observation, Discernment and the Limits of Knowledge*

> “If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles.”
>
> **Sun Tzu, *The Art of War*, Chapter III**

## 1.1 Information Is Not Intelligence

The novice operative is inclined to believe that intelligence consists of secrets: hidden documents, overheard conversations, intercepted correspondence and the confidential deliberations of powerful men.

This is a common misunderstanding.

Secrets are merely one source of information, and secrecy is no guarantee of value. A confidential report may be inaccurate, a carefully guarded document may be obsolete, and an apparently trivial observation may possess considerable strategic significance.

Information becomes intelligence when it has been evaluated, placed in context and made useful to a decision.

Consider a merchant who reports that six wagons carrying grain departed a provincial town before dawn.

This is information.

An examination of the town's commercial records reveals that no grain shipment was scheduled. A second source reports that the wagons travelled under military escort. A third observes that the local garrison has recently increased its purchase of provisions.

Taken together, these observations may indicate preparations for a military operation.

This is intelligence.

The distinction is not merely academic. Those who collect information without examining its significance become repositories of disconnected facts. Those who interpret information without adequate evidence become manufacturers of speculation.

The accomplished intelligence officer must guard against both errors.

## 1.2 The Value of Foreknowledge

Intelligence derives much of its value from time.

To learn that an enemy army has crossed a border is useful. To learn that it intends to cross the border in three days may be decisive.

A warning received after an assassination is evidence. The same warning received beforehand may prevent the murder.

Yet information does not possess a fixed value. Its usefulness depends upon the circumstances in which it is acquired and the decisions it can influence.

The location of a concealed passage may be worthless to a merchant and invaluable to a prisoner. The identity of a minor court official may seem inconsequential until that official receives responsibility for approving military requisitions.

Thus, the value of intelligence cannot be determined solely by the apparent importance of its subject.

The operative must continually ask:

- What does this information tell us?
- What decision might it influence?
- Who requires it?
- How reliable is it?
- How long will it remain useful?
- What might happen if it is incorrect?

An intelligence report that cannot inform a decision may still merit preservation, but it should not be mistaken for an operational advantage.

The mere accumulation of knowledge is not the purpose of the trade.

**The purpose of intelligence is to reduce uncertainty sufficiently to permit informed action.**

## 1.3 The Three States of Knowledge

Every intelligence assessment must distinguish between three fundamentally different conditions.

**What is known.** Facts supported by evidence of sufficient reliability that they may reasonably be accepted as established.

**What is believed.** Conclusions drawn from available evidence, but subject to uncertainty, alternative explanations or incomplete corroboration.

**What remains unknown.** Questions for which sufficient evidence has not been obtained.

Confusing these conditions is among the most dangerous failures of intelligence practice.

Suppose an operative learns that a senior Imperial official has visited a remote Solar monastery on three occasions during the past month.

The visits may be established through reliable observation.

The operative may believe that the official is supervising a clandestine operation.

But the purpose of the visits remains unknown.

It would be improper to report the suspected operation as fact merely because the hypothesis appears plausible.

A disciplined report might instead state:

*The official has visited the monastery on three confirmed occasions. The visits coincide with increased security and the arrival of unregistered supplies. These observations are consistent with clandestine activity, although the nature of that activity remains undetermined.*

Such language may seem unnecessarily cautious to those who demand certainty. It is, however, the language upon which sound decisions are built.

An operative who conceals uncertainty does not strengthen an assessment. He deprives its recipient of the ability to judge the risks of acting upon it.

### Confidence Is Not Certainty

Confidence expresses the strength of an assessment, not an assurance that the conclusion is true.

An assessment may be held with high confidence because it is supported by several reliable, independent sources. Another may be held with low confidence because it depends upon a single unverified account.

Neither condition guarantees correctness.

The intelligence officer must remain prepared to revise even a strongly held conclusion when new evidence warrants it.

There is no dishonour in discovering that an earlier assessment was mistaken.

There is considerable danger in refusing to acknowledge the mistake.

## 1.4 Sources and Their Limitations

Intelligence may be acquired through many means.

A conversation with a servant, an intercepted message, a merchant's ledger, an examination of disturbed earth, an aerial observation or a divinatory vision may each contribute useful evidence.

These methods differ considerably, but all share one limitation: **a source can reveal only what lies within its capacity to know or observe.**

A palace servant may reliably describe the arrival of visitors without understanding the purpose of their meetings.

A captured officer may accurately report the orders he received while remaining ignorant of the deception practised upon his own command.

A merchant may provide truthful accounts of unusual purchases without knowing that those purchases were intended to mislead observers.

Even the most trustworthy witness can be mistaken.

It is therefore necessary to distinguish between the reliability of a source and the credibility of the particular information provided.

A source who has consistently supplied accurate information may nevertheless report an unfounded rumour. Conversely, an unreliable source may occasionally speak the truth.

The prudent operative evaluates both independently.

### Corroboration and the Illusion of Agreement

Information obtained from multiple sources is generally more useful than information obtained from one, provided those sources are genuinely independent.

Three witnesses who observed the same event may provide valuable corroboration.

Three witnesses who repeat a story originating from the same tavern keeper do not.

The distinction is frequently overlooked.

An adversary skilled in deception may deliberately introduce false information into several channels, creating the appearance of independent confirmation.

The investigator must therefore examine not merely whether accounts agree, but whether they may share a common origin.

Agreement is evidence only to the extent that its causes are understood.

### An Exercise in Remembering

An instructor places a cracked measure, two dried roots, a brass weight and several other small articles on an apothecary's tray. After a brief inspection, she covers it and asks the pupil to describe what was there. The pupil must distinguish what he remembers from what he merely expects an apothecary to possess.

When the cloth is lifted, compare the account with the objects. An invented third root matters as much as an overlooked weight. On another attempt, ask a particular question before showing the tray: which articles appear damaged? Notice what the question helps the pupil retain and what falls outside his attention.

The exercise gives a modest way to examine one's habits of observation. Success with a familiar tray does not establish that a hurried account of an unfamiliar room is complete. A witness who remembers little and admits it may be more useful than one who fills every gap.

## 1.5 Intelligence and Deception

Intelligence is collected in an environment where other parties may actively seek to conceal, distort or fabricate the truth.

An army may establish false camps to disguise its movements. A merchant may circulate rumours to influence the price of grain. A court official may leak authentic documents selected to support a misleading conclusion.

The most effective deception does not necessarily rely upon falsehood.

A truthful observation, presented without its proper context, may produce an entirely mistaken interpretation.

Consider a monastery that receives a substantial shipment of iron restraints.

One observer concludes that prisoners are being held there.

Another discovers that the monastery operates a foundry and uses the ironwork in its machinery.

A third learns that the restraints were delivered to the foundry but transferred elsewhere during the night.

Each discovery changes the significance of the original observation.

An experienced adversary may deliberately exploit the natural inclination of investigators to settle upon the first plausible explanation.

The operative must therefore cultivate the habit of considering alternative interpretations, especially when evidence appears to confirm an existing suspicion.

**The intelligence officer's greatest adversary is not always the enemy's deception, but his own desire to be correct.**

## 1.6 The Problem of Magical Knowledge

The existence of divination, telepathy and other supernatural arts has led some practitioners to suppose that magical intelligence is inherently more reliable than information obtained through ordinary means.

This confidence is misplaced.

Magic may extend the reach of perception, but it does not necessarily extend the judgment of the observer.

An operative observing a distant chamber through a scrying instrument may see events accurately while misunderstanding their significance.

A telepath may perceive a sincerely held belief that is nevertheless false.

An enchanted object may disclose the presence of magic without explaining its purpose.

A spell that reveals the location of a person does not establish that person's intentions, loyalties or knowledge.

The distinction between observation and interpretation remains intact.

### The Limits of Divination

Each magical discipline possesses particular capabilities and limitations.

The competent practitioner must understand these limitations before relying upon the information obtained.

A divination that answers a narrowly framed question may provide an accurate response while leaving the larger problem unresolved.

A vision may depict an event without revealing its causes.

A supernatural warning may indicate danger without identifying its source.

Furthermore, the existence of magical observation necessarily encourages the development of magical concealment.

An adversary who knows that divination is possible may employ wards, illusions, decoys or other protective measures. The absence of a magical indication must not therefore be confused with evidence that nothing is present.

Nor should an operative presume that a magical method is secure simply because its operation is invisible to ordinary senses.

The same arts that permit supernatural observation may permit the detection of supernatural interference.

### Knowledge Without a Knower

There is a further distinction of considerable importance.

A mind may possess knowledge of a cipher's key. A document containing encrypted writing does not.

The written symbols exist upon the parchment, but their interpretation depends upon information that may exist elsewhere.

A spell capable of understanding an unfamiliar language need not therefore possess the ability to decipher an encrypted message.

Likewise, a spell capable of examining an object cannot be assumed to recover information that the object itself does not contain.

This distinction has substantial consequences for the protection of intelligence.

Mundane cryptography, properly employed, may provide safeguards that elaborate enchantments cannot reproduce. Conversely, a perfectly encrypted message may be compromised through the careless handling of its key.

The prudent intelligence officer employs magic where it provides an advantage, but never mistakes supernatural power for a substitute for sound method.

## 1.7 Intelligence Requirements

Before information is collected, the operative should establish what must be learned and why.

This is known as an intelligence requirement.

A poorly framed requirement might instruct an agent to discover everything possible about a particular monastery.

Such an instruction offers little guidance. It encourages indiscriminate collection, wastes resources and exposes sources to unnecessary risk.

A more useful requirement might ask:

*Does the monastery maintain a concealed detention facility, and if so, who controls access to it?*

This question identifies an uncertainty whose resolution could inform a particular course of action.

It may then be divided into supporting questions.

What persons or supplies enter the monastery? Are its official records consistent with its observed activities? Does its architecture suggest concealed accommodation? Have individuals been transferred there without subsequent public record?

Different questions may require different methods of collection.

A local merchant might provide information about deliveries. A former servant might describe the internal arrangements. Public records may identify discrepancies in expenditure. Magical reconnaissance may reveal features inaccessible to ordinary observation.

The collection effort should follow the requirement, rather than allowing the availability of a particular method to dictate what is investigated.

This principle is especially important when powerful magical capabilities are available.

The possession of a divinatory instrument does not make every intelligence problem a question for divination.

## 1.8 The Cost of Knowing

Intelligence is never without cost.

The obvious expenditures are coin, equipment, time and personnel. Less visible are the costs imposed upon sources, relationships and operational security.

An inquiry may alert an adversary to the existence of an investigation.

A source may be exposed through an apparently harmless request.

A magical observation may leave evidence of interference.

An intercepted communication may reveal the existence of a clandestine network even when its contents remain concealed.

Every collection activity must therefore be weighed against its expected value and the risks it creates.

This does not imply that intelligence gathering should be timid. Excessive caution may be as damaging as recklessness.

Rather, the operative should be able to explain why the information is needed, what obtaining it may require, and what consequences may follow.

There will be circumstances in which a particular secret is not worth the danger of acquiring it.

There will also be circumstances in which the failure to acquire that secret will cost many lives.

Sound judgment consists in recognising the difference.

## 1.9 The Discipline of Doubt

Intelligence work demands curiosity, but curiosity alone is insufficient.

The operative must cultivate a particular form of disciplined doubt.

He must be prepared to question an informant without insulting him, challenge an assessment without dismissing its author, and reconsider a conclusion without abandoning the pursuit of truth.

He must distinguish between an absence of evidence and evidence of absence.

He must recognise that an adversary may be competent, that an ally may be mistaken, and that his own expectations may influence what he notices and what he overlooks.

Above all, he must learn to identify the moment at which a plausible explanation begins to acquire the unwarranted authority of an established fact.

This discipline does not require perpetual indecision.

The purpose of intelligence is to support action, and action will often be necessary before uncertainty can be eliminated.

An effective intelligence officer must therefore be capable of reaching provisional conclusions, communicating their limitations and revising them when circumstances change.

The operative who refuses to act until every question is answered will accomplish little.

The operative who acts as though no questions remain unanswered may accomplish considerably worse.

## 1.10 Principles for the Practitioner

The foundations of sound intelligence practice may be summarised in ten principles.

1. **Begin with a question.** Determine what must be learned before deciding how to collect it.
2. **Distinguish information from intelligence.** A fact acquires operational value through relevance, context and interpretation.
3. **Separate knowledge from belief.** Report uncertainty rather than concealing it.
4. **Evaluate both source and claim.** A reliable person can be mistaken, and an unreliable person can speak the truth.
5. **Seek independent corroboration.** Agreement between accounts is meaningful only when their origins are understood.
6. **Consider alternative explanations.** Evidence that supports one conclusion may also support another.
7. **Respect the limitations of magic.** Supernatural observation does not guarantee understanding.
8. **Collect with purpose.** Every inquiry consumes resources and may create exposure.
9. **Preserve the ability to revise.** A conclusion must remain answerable to new evidence.
10. **Remember that intelligence serves decisions.** Knowledge pursued without regard to its use is scholarship, not necessarily intelligence.

These principles should inform every subsequent discipline described in this manual, whether the operative is cultivating a human source, intercepting correspondence, examining a suspicious transaction or conducting clandestine reconnaissance.

## Closing Observation

An owl can distinguish the movement of a mouse in darkness. It possesses extraordinary powers of observation, yet it knows nothing of the hunter who has placed a snare beneath its perch.

To see is not necessarily to understand.

To understand is not necessarily to know.

And to know is not necessarily to act wisely.

The accomplished intelligence officer must learn all three disciplines: observation, judgment and action.

The chapters that follow describe the methods by which these disciplines are practised.

*In the invisible trade, certainty is a luxury. Judgment is a necessity.*
