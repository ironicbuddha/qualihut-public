# Introduction
## The Necessity of Knowing

> “Knowledge of the enemy's dispositions can only be obtained from other men.”
>
> **Sun Tzu, *The Art of War*, Chapter XIII**

Throughout history, kingdoms have risen and fallen, armies have marched to victory or ruin, and rulers have lost their thrones, not merely through superiority of arms, but through the possession or absence of knowledge.

A general who knows where his enemy will strike may defeat an army twice his size. A merchant who knows that war is coming may preserve his fortune while his competitors are ruined. A ruler who knows of a conspiracy before its conspirators act may prevent a rebellion without drawing a sword.

In each case, the decisive advantage is not strength, wealth or magical power. It is knowledge acquired before the moment at which it is needed.

**Intelligence is the discipline of acquiring that advantage.**

### The Limits of Power

The inexperienced operative often mistakes capability for security.

A warrior trusts his sword. A wizard trusts his spells. A noble trusts his title, and a wealthy man his gold. Each may possess considerable power, yet none of these advantages protects against a danger of which its possessor remains ignorant.

The finest sword is of little use against an assassin whose presence is unsuspected. A fortress enchanted against intrusion offers scant protection when its commander has been deceived. An army may be victorious in every engagement and still lose a war because its enemies understand its intentions better than it understands theirs.

Magic does not abolish this problem.

A diviner may observe a distant chamber without knowing the significance of what is witnessed. An enchantment may compel a truthful answer to the wrong question. A messenger may cross a thousand leagues in an instant, carrying intelligence that was false before it was dispatched.

Nor should the practitioner assume that supernatural means are inherently superior to mundane ones. The testimony of a reliable servant may be worth more than an elaborate divination. A properly encrypted letter may conceal its meaning more effectively than an enchanted seal. A conversation between friends may reveal intentions that no spell was designed to uncover.

Power provides the means to act. Intelligence provides the grounds upon which action may be wisely chosen.

### The Invisible Contest

Behind the visible contests of armies, courts, commerce and religion lies another struggle, conducted largely beyond public observation.

Its participants are not always soldiers, nor do they necessarily bear weapons.

They may be diplomats, merchants, scholars, priests, servants, travellers or entertainers. They may occupy positions of great authority or possess so little social standing that their presence goes almost entirely unnoticed.

Their value lies not in their rank, but in what they know, what they can discover, and what they can cause others to believe.

A servant who overhears an indiscreet conversation may possess knowledge worth more than the treasury of his master. A cartographer's correction to an obsolete map may expose a weakness in the defences of a city. A seemingly inconsequential alteration to a shipping manifest may reveal the movement of an army.

Such fragments rarely announce their importance.

Intelligence work therefore demands more than the collection of secrets. It requires the patience to recognise significance, the judgment to distinguish truth from falsehood, and the discipline to act without revealing what has been learned.

The contest is seldom between those who possess knowledge and those who possess none. More often, it is between competing interpretations of incomplete information.

The successful operative must understand not only what an adversary knows, but what that adversary believes, what remains unknown to them, and what they may mistakenly regard as certain.

### The Human Element

Whatever instruments are employed in the gathering of intelligence, the human element remains indispensable.

Men and women possess ambitions, loyalties, grievances, affections, prejudices and fears. They keep secrets for reasons that are seldom as simple as obedience or reward. They reveal those secrets through carelessness, conviction, misplaced confidence, friendship or deliberate betrayal.

It is tempting to regard such individuals merely as sources from which information may be extracted. This is an error both practical and moral.

A person is not a document to be opened, examined and returned to its shelf.

Every relationship exists within a web of other relationships. Every confidence has consequences. Every act of cooperation may place obligations upon both parties, whether those obligations are acknowledged or not.

An operative who understands only how to obtain information, but not how to preserve the trust of those who provide it, will eventually find himself rich in secrets and poor in allies.

The most enduring intelligence networks are not necessarily those maintained by the greatest expenditure of coin or the most persuasive threats. They are often those whose members possess sufficient reason to continue cooperating when obedience becomes inconvenient and danger becomes real.

### The Burden of Secrecy

The practice of intelligence carries obligations that are not always apparent to those who undertake it.

Secrets must be protected, yet secrecy may conceal incompetence as readily as necessity. Deception may prevent bloodshed, yet it may also become a justification for acts that would otherwise be indefensible. Loyalty is indispensable, but unquestioning loyalty can make an operative the instrument of another's folly.

There will be occasions when the interests of a patron conflict with the welfare of an informant, when the truth threatens an established allegiance, or when the apparent success of an operation conceals a greater failure.

No manual can resolve every such conflict.

It can, however, provide the principles by which those conflicts may be recognised and their consequences understood.

An operative must accept responsibility not only for the information acquired, but for the methods employed, the people endangered and the decisions that follow.

Secrecy may shield an action from public scrutiny. It does not absolve its author of responsibility.

### The Purpose of This Manual

The Invisible Trade is concerned with the acquisition, protection, interpretation and application of intelligence.

Its methods encompass the cultivation of human relationships, the observation of adversaries, the interception of communications, the examination of records, the protection of secrets and the organisation of clandestine networks.

Attention is also given to the employment of magical arts, their limitations, and the means by which their advantages may be resisted or overcome.

The reader should not mistake the practices described herein for an infallible collection of procedures.

Circumstances vary. Adversaries adapt. Methods that succeed in one city may prove disastrous in another. The most sophisticated operation may fail through a trivial oversight, while an inexperienced agent may succeed through an unexpected opportunity.

The purpose of training is not to eliminate uncertainty. Such an ambition is impossible.

It is to develop the habits of observation, inquiry, judgment and restraint by which uncertainty may be reduced to a degree that permits informed action.

The competent operative learns the rules of his profession.

The accomplished operative understands why those rules exist, when they apply, and when circumstances demand that they be reconsidered.

Above all, the practitioner of the invisible trade must resist the most dangerous temptation of the intelligence profession: the belief that possession of information is equivalent to possession of truth.

**What is known, what is believed, and what is true are three different things.**

The discipline of intelligence begins with understanding the distinction.
